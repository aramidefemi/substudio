How you subtitle a film

Taken from your screen recording. For each step: what you do today in Subtitle Edit, and how the new app could do it.

Today Proposed Biggest time saving
  1. 01

    Open the film

    Today

    New file, then Video › Open video. Previews come watermarked, filed by client. Any script lives in Google Docs, in another window.

    Proposed

    One screen takes the video, script and any old .srt. Frame rate and reading limits are remembered per client.

  2. 02

    First draft

    Today

    There is no draft. Every cue starts empty and you build all of them by hand, often 1,000+ per film.

    Proposed

    Optional AI draft: the Yoruba it heard, an English line and timing for every stretch of speech. You can start editing while it finishes.

  3. 03

    Translate & write

    Today

    Listen in Yoruba and type the English from scratch, cue by cue, e.g. "(Singing)", "In Jesus' name."

    Proposed

    Review instead of type: press 1–3 for a suggestion, reuse your own past wording, or type over it. Enter approves and moves on.

  4. 04

    Time each cue

    Today

    Click the waveform for a 2-second cue, then drag its edges by hand while replaying, down to the frame.

    Proposed

    Cues already sit on the speech. "Snap to speech" does the rough drag; the same waveform and frame nudges stay for fine work.

  5. 05

    Check & export

    Today

    Red cells flag problems without saying why. You save the .srt through File › Save.

    Proposed

    Problems listed in plain words with one-click fixes, then export. Your approved lines become your private translation memory, if you agree.

About this mockup

  • The Yoruba lines are sample dialogue, so please correct the wording and tone marks.
  • Cue counts, film names and client presets are placeholders.
  • Limits shown: 42 characters per line, 17 characters per second, 7 seconds max on screen.
  • The editor copies Subtitle Edit's layout on purpose, so your habits still work.
